## The Pasteurization Question: Is Good Culture Made With Pasteurized Milk?

**Yes.** Good Culture cottage cheese is made with **pasteurized skim milk and cream.** This is confirmed by the ingredient list on every container and is consistent with FDA requirements for commercially sold cottage cheese in the United States. All milk used in Good Culture products undergoes pasteurization before it enters the culturing process.

Pasteurization is the process of heating milk to a specific temperature for a specific duration to **kill harmful bacteria**, including *Listeria monocytogenes*, *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, and *Campylobacter*. These are the pathogens that pose the greatest risk during pregnancy. When milk is properly pasteurized, these organisms are destroyed, and the resulting dairy product is safe for consumption by pregnant women, immunocompromised individuals, and the general population.

The confusion about dairy safety during pregnancy stems from the fact that **not all cheeses are made with pasteurized milk.** Some artisanal and imported cheeses — particularly soft varieties like brie, camembert, queso fresco, and certain blue cheeses — may be made with raw (unpasteurized) milk. Raw milk can harbor *Listeria* and other pathogens even when the cheese appears and smells normal. This is why pregnancy dietary guidelines distinguish between pasteurized and unpasteurized dairy products.

Good Culture falls firmly on the **safe side of that distinction.** It is a commercially produced, pasteurized-milk cottage cheese with no raw milk components.

### What "Pasteurized" Actually Means for Listeria Risk

*Listeria monocytogenes* is the bacterium that drives most pregnancy-related food restrictions around dairy. Unlike many other foodborne pathogens, *Listeria* can grow at refrigerator temperatures, which means it can multiply in cold foods like cottage cheese if the product is contaminated after pasteurization.

Pasteurization eliminates *Listeria* present in the raw milk. The risk then shifts to **post-pasteurization contamination** — the possibility that the bacteria enter the product during packaging, handling, or after the consumer opens the container. This is not unique to Good Culture; it applies to all pasteurized dairy products.

The key takeaway: pasteurization makes the product safe at the time of manufacture. **Your handling and storage practices** determine whether it remains safe until you finish the container.

## Safe Storage Practices for Pregnant Women

Proper storage is the single most important thing you can do to maintain the safety of any cottage cheese during pregnancy. The rules are straightforward but non-negotiable.

### Refrigerator Temperature

Your refrigerator should be set to **40°F (4°C) or below.** At this temperature, *Listeria* growth is significantly slowed (though not completely stopped). Many home refrigerators run warmer than their owners realize — a dedicated refrigerator thermometer costs a few dollars and provides peace of mind that the number on the dial matches the actual temperature inside.

Store cottage cheese on a **middle or lower shelf**, not in the door. The door is the warmest part of the refrigerator because it is opened frequently, causing temperature fluctuations. A consistent, cold environment extends the safe life of the product.

### Unopened Containers

An unopened container of Good Culture cottage cheese is safe **until the printed expiration or best-by date**, provided it has been continuously refrigerated at the proper temperature. The sealed packaging maintains an environment that inhibits bacterial growth, and the pasteurization process ensures the product starts clean.

Do not purchase containers that are **damaged, swollen, or leaking.** A compromised seal can allow post-pasteurization contamination. If a container looks unusual on the shelf, choose a different one.

### After Opening: The Critical Window

Once you open a container of Good Culture cottage cheese, the clock starts ticking. The product is now exposed to air, utensils, and the general environment of your refrigerator — all potential sources of bacterial contamination.

The safe window for consumption after opening is **five to seven days**, stored properly at 40°F or below. For pregnant women, erring on the side of **five days** is the more conservative and safer choice.

Transfer any leftover cottage cheese to a **clean, airtight container** if you do not plan to eat it directly from the original packaging. This minimizes air exposure and reduces the chance of cross-contamination from other foods in the refrigerator.

**Never return a spoon that has touched your mouth, other food, or a countertop back into the cottage cheese container.** This is one of the most common ways bacteria are introduced into dairy products. Use a clean spoon every time you serve yourself.

## Signs of Spoilage: When to Throw It Away Immediately

Even within the recommended consumption window, cottage cheese can spoil prematurely if storage conditions are not ideal. Knowing the warning signs protects you from eating a product that has become unsafe.

### Visual Changes

Fresh Good Culture cottage cheese has a **uniform white to slightly off-white color** with visible but evenly distributed curds. If you notice any of the following, discard the entire container:

**Pink, yellow, or orange discoloration** indicates bacterial or mold growth. Even a small spot of unusual color means the entire container should be thrown away — not just the discolored portion, as bacteria can spread invisibly through the product.

**Visible mold** on the surface or around the lid is an immediate discard signal. Mold produces mycotoxins that can be harmful during pregnancy, and surface mold indicates that the underlying product has already been compromised.

**Excessive liquid separation** beyond normal whey pooling may indicate that the product has been stored at improper temperatures or is past its safe window. While some whey separation is normal, a large volume of watery liquid mixed with disintegrating curds suggests deterioration.

### Smell Changes

Fresh cottage cheese has a **mild, slightly tangy aroma** consistent with its cultured nature. If the smell shifts to anything sour, ammonia-like, putrid, or simply "off" in a way that is different from when you first opened it, **trust your nose and discard the product.** Olfactory detection is one of the most reliable indicators of bacterial overgrowth in dairy.

### Texture Changes

Good Culture's characteristic texture is **creamy with small, uniform curds.** If the curds become slimy, excessively mushy, or develop a gritty or grainy texture that was not present when fresh, the product has likely begun to break down from bacterial activity and should not be consumed.

Beyond safety, Good Culture offers genuine nutritional advantages for pregnant women who can tolerate it well.

Good Culture provides meaningful calcium per serving, supporting the **development of the fetal skeletal system.** During pregnancy, if maternal calcium intake is insufficient, the body draws calcium from the mother's own bones to meet the baby's needs — a process that can compromise the mother's long-term bone health.

Pairing cottage cheese with vitamin D-rich foods (or a prenatal vitamin containing vitamin D) enhances calcium absorption and utilization.

## When to Avoid Cottage Cheese During Pregnancy

Despite its overall safety profile, there are specific situations where avoiding cottage cheese — even a pasteurized brand like Good Culture — is the prudent choice.

### During Active Gastrointestinal Illness

If you are experiencing vomiting, diarrhea, or other symptoms of a stomach virus, dairy products including cottage cheese may worsen symptoms and are harder to digest during acute illness. Wait until symptoms resolve before reintroducing dairy.

### If You Have Been Diagnosed With Listeriosis

Listeriosis during pregnancy is rare but serious. If you have been diagnosed or are being tested for listeria infection, your healthcare provider will give you specific dietary restrictions that supersede general guidance. Follow their instructions precisely.

### If the Product Has Been Temperature-Abused

If cottage cheese has been left out of the refrigerator for **more than two hours** (or more than one hour in temperatures above 90°F), it should be discarded regardless of how it looks or smells. Bacterial growth accelerates rapidly at room temperature, and the product may be unsafe even if no spoilage signs are visible.

This applies to cottage cheese that was left on the counter during meal prep, carried in a lunch bag without adequate ice packs, or accidentally left out overnight. When in doubt, throw it out.

### If You Have a Dairy Allergy or Severe Lactose Intolerance

Good Culture is not suitable for women with a **true dairy allergy** (an immune-mediated reaction to milk proteins). Lactose intolerance is different from a dairy allergy — many lactose-intolerant women tolerate Good Culture well due to its live cultures and fermentation process — but a true allergy requires complete dairy avoidance regardless of the product's quality or processing.

## Reading Labels: What to Check Every Time You Buy

Developing a quick label-checking habit ensures that every container of cottage cheese you buy during pregnancy meets safety standards.

**Verify pasteurization.** The ingredient list should state "pasteurized milk" or "pasteurized skim milk." Good Culture consistently lists pasteurized milk, but it is worth confirming, especially if you try a different brand or product line.

**Check the expiration date.** Choose the container with the **latest date available** on the shelf. Do not purchase products that are within a day or two of expiration if you plan to eat them over several days.

**Inspect the container.** Look for intact seals, no swelling or bloating of the packaging, no visible damage, and no leaking. A compromised container may have been exposed to contamination.

**Review storage temperature.** The cottage cheese section of the grocery store should feel cold to the touch. If products in the dairy case feel warm or if there is an unusual amount of condensation on containers, the cold chain may have been broken during stocking.
